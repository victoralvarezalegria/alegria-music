How I Went From Lips That Stopped Vibrating at 17 to Principal Trombone of the Helsinki Philharmonic

I grew up in Santander, a small city in the north of Spain.

No professional orchestra. No higher conservatory. Nobody in my house had an orchestra career or a roadmap to hand me.

I started the trombone at 9. My dream was to study with my hero, Jörgen van Rijen, in Amsterdam.

My friends told me to forget it. "It's not for you. Maybe in another life."

Then my mother, who is not a musician, said the sentence that stayed with me for years.

"A worker does eight, nine, ten hours a day at a normal job. Why don't you do the same?"

So I did. 8 in the morning to 8 at night. Only trombone. No piano. No singing. No listening to recordings.

Same warm-up, same books, excerpts over and over again. When I sounded worse, I played more to fix it.

Then, months before the audition of my life, a sharp pain. My lips stopped vibrating.

I was 17.

I almost ended my career before it even started.

I had to stop playing. I was terrified I would never play trombone again.

The lip came back. Not at 100%, but enough. I got into the Conservatorium van Amsterdam, in Jörgen's class.

With Jörgen I changed how I practiced. Efficient. Never exhausted. And I started improving again, this time in a healthy way.

Then came the auditions. Orchestra finals, again and again.

Final, final, final. But never the seat.

Close enough to touch it. Still a whole world away.

Then COVID came. Everything stopped, and I was stuck again.

But one voice inside me kept saying the same thing. You have to play with the Berlin Philharmonic.

At the Karajan Academy audition of the Berlin Philharmonic, I reached the final with one other player, and they gave the seat to nobody. "The level is not high enough."

They gave us feedback that day. I was the only one writing it down.

And the feedback surprised me. In Amsterdam, Jörgen taught me that in William Tell the long note was everything. In Berlin, Olaf Ott told me the opposite: give the intention to the short notes.

My playing didn't need to change. My approach did.

So I spent July and August locked in my room in Amsterdam while my colleagues posted photos from Rome and the beach.

This time it wasn't only trombone. Some trombone. Then recordings. Then a run. Then my old lessons with Jörgen on repeat.

The best players in the world don't practice more than you. They practice differently.

At the next audition, I won the Karajan Academy of the Berlin Philharmonic.

Two seasons. More than 20 projects on the stage of the Philharmonie.

Then the last months of the Academy came, and I still had no orchestra job. Close again. No seat.

For Helsinki I went all in on the new system. Half an hour of warm-up. Then piano. Then scales and etudes. A walk in the park with my lesson recordings. The gym.

Every day for 90 days, I sat in a chair and lived the whole audition in my head. The flight. The hotel. The warm-up room. Every round. And at the end, I always won.

And I won Principal Trombone of the Helsinki Philharmonic.

In every audition I won, I was never the best player in the room.

Not the best high register. Not the best sound. Not even close.

None of it was talent. It all came from the method I found at my lowest point, when my lips stopped vibrating.

Then I started posting what I practice. Musicians from all over the world started asking me for lessons.

And I saw my old problems in every one of them. Hours with no progress. Tired chops. Great in the practice room, blocked on stage.

So I took everything I learned from Jörgen van Rijen, Stefan Schulz and Joseph Alessi, and built one system.

A one-year program where I guide you personally, the same way the players in Berlin guided me.

It's called the 1% Musician Accelerator.

The Four-Part System That Took Me From Final Rounds to Principal Trombone

How you practice, how you perform, how you play, and how you get opportunities. Every one of these matters as much as the others.

  1. 01
    1% Practice Method

    Play Much Better on Fewer Hours

    More hours equals more progress. That myth cost me my lip at 17. Without a system, the hours give you nothing.

    You practice the way the best brass players in the world told me to, with a big part of the work away from the horn: singing, conducting, mental practice, piano and my 40/60 method.

    You stay fresh in the head and in the lips. Sixty to ninety focused minutes beat four blind hours.

  2. 02
    1% Stage Conquer Method

    Play on Stage the Way You Play in the Practice Room

    In the practice room, everything works. On stage, the nerves take over. That's not because you're bad. It's because you have never practiced under pressure.

    So you stop only practicing. You perform, for other people, as if it was the day of the audition, over and over again.

    Owning the stage is not a talent. It's a method, and you can train it. Your worst day starts beating their best day.

  3. 03
    1% Embouchure Technique

    An Embouchure Built for Your Face, Not Your Teacher's

    When I started, I copied my teacher's embouchure. It was not made for me, and it almost cost me my career.

    Your teeth, your jaw and the size of your lips are your own. Small adjustments give you an embouchure that fits you, so you stop fighting the instrument.

    The high register comes from the work you do in the middle and low registers, not from pushing. Stefan Schulz taught me that in Berlin. The result: high notes that are there every day, not there one day and gone the next.

  4. 04
    1% Career Accelerator

    Get Recognized for the Hours You've Put In

    You can play better than anyone in the building. If nobody knows who you are, nobody calls you. Not for a masterclass, not for a recital, not for a project.

    I'm not teaching you what worked in 1986. I'm teaching you what works now: how to make yourself valuable in the music world, so festivals, orchestras and the players you admire reach out to you.
